Learn the basics

Short lessons on the ideas behind investing, in plain words. Each takes a minute or two. When a lesson mentions something you can see for yourself, there is a link to try it with pretend money.

Not financial advice. This app is for learning and practice only. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell anything, past results do not predict future ones, and real investing carries real risk. For decisions about your own money, talk to a qualified, independent adviser.

1. Stocks

A stock (or share) is a small piece of ownership in a company. If the company grows and earns more, its shares tend to become worth more, and some companies also pay part of their profits to shareholders as dividends.

The price of a stock moves every day as buyers and sellers change their minds about the company's future. A single company can do badly or even fail, so one stock on its own can lose most or all of its value.

Stocks have grown more than most other investments over long periods, but they are also the bumpiest ride.

2. Bonds

A bond is a loan. You lend money to a government or company; they pay you interest and give the money back on a set date. Because the payments are agreed in advance, bonds usually move much less than stocks.

Bonds are not risk-free. When interest rates rise, older bonds paying less become worth less, which is what happened in 2022. A borrower can also fail to pay.

Bonds usually grow more slowly than stocks, and they often hold up better when stocks fall, which is why many portfolios mix the two.

3. Funds and ETFs

A fund pools money from many people and buys many investments at once. One fund can hold hundreds or thousands of stocks or bonds, so buying one share of it spreads your money widely.

An index fund simply buys everything in a list such as the S&P 500 (500 large US companies), rather than paying someone to pick winners. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a fund you buy and sell during the day like a stock; a mutual fund is priced once a day. SPY and VOO are ETFs; VTSAX is a mutual fund. Many hold nearly the same things.

For most beginners, a few broad, low-cost index funds do the job of hundreds of individual picks.

Try it: see example portfolios built from funds.

4. Diversification

Diversification means not putting all your eggs in one basket. If you own many different investments, one of them collapsing hurts a little instead of a lot.

It works best when the things you own don't all move together: large and small companies, US and other countries, stocks and bonds. Owning ten tech companies is less diverse than it looks, because they tend to rise and fall together.

Diversifying won't stop the whole market from falling, but it greatly lowers the chance that one bad pick sinks you.

5. Fees

Funds charge a yearly fee called the expense ratio, taken out of your money quietly. It looks tiny, but it is taken every year, on everything you have, for as long as you hold it.

Over 30 years, $10,000 growing at 7% a year becomes about $76,000. With a 1% yearly fee it grows at 6% instead and becomes about $57,000: the fee costs about a quarter of the result. A 0.05% fee costs about $1,000.

Fees are one of the few things you control. Lower is almost always better.

6. Volatility and drawdown

Volatility is how much a price jumps around. A volatility of 15% a year means that in a typical year the value swings roughly 15% above or below its trend.

Drawdown is the fall from a high point to the low that follows. In 2008–2009 the S&P 500 fell about 55% from its peak and took over four years to get back. The question drawdown asks is: could you watch half your money vanish on paper and not sell?

Higher growth usually comes with bigger drops. Choose a mix whose worst drop you could live through without panicking.

7. Time in the market

Nobody reliably knows when the market will rise or fall. Many of the best days come right after the worst ones, so people who sell in a panic and wait for things to "settle down" often miss the recovery.

Over a single year, stocks lose money fairly often. Over 15 or 20 years, a broad stock fund has historically almost always ended higher. Time smooths out the bumps, though it can't promise anything.

"Time in the market beats timing the market": staying invested has usually mattered more than when you got in.

Try it: see how a portfolio did over different stretches.

8. Dollar-cost averaging

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) means investing a fixed amount on a schedule, say every month, whatever the price. When prices are low your money buys more; when they are high it buys less. Saving from each paycheck is DCA by nature.

If you already have a lump of money, the question is whether to invest it all now or spread it out. Because markets rise more often than they fall, investing all at once has won roughly two times out of three historically. Spreading it out wins when a fall comes soon after you start, and it can make it easier to begin.

DCA is a good habit for new savings. For money you already have, it trades some expected growth for less regret if a crash comes right away.

Try it: compare all at once with spreading it out.

9. Rebalancing

Say you choose 60% stocks and 40% bonds. After a few good years for stocks you might have 75% stocks without doing anything: the portfolio has become riskier than you chose. Rebalancing means selling some of what grew and buying what lagged, to get back to 60/40.

People usually rebalance once a year, or when the mix drifts more than a few percent. It makes you sell a little high and buy a little low, but its main job is to keep your risk where you set it, not to raise returns.

Pick a mix you're comfortable with, then rebalance now and then to keep it.
